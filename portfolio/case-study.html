Case study · Remote sensing and machine learning

Classi-fy

A model that spots oil palm plantations in the Congo Basin from satellite imagery.

The use

What it makes possible

Knowing where oil palm plantations are is a prerequisite for almost everything: tracking deforestation, auditing a concession, deciding where to send a team. In the Democratic Republic of the Congo that information exists nowhere in a reliable, up-to-date form, and the country is far too large to check everywhere on foot.

Classi-fy narrows the field. For a given plot, it reports the probability that oil palm is present, using public and freely available satellite imagery.

The main use is prioritisation. Rather than visiting a hundred sites at random, you rank them by decreasing probability and start with the ones that most deserve attention. A team with ten field trips available spends them better.

The model produces a signal, not a verdict. It replaces no verification, it says where to look first.

The problem

Why this is hard

Seen by an optical satellite, which measures the light reflected off the ground, an oil palm plantation and a dense tropical forest look remarkably alike. Both are closed canopies, green all year, and their greenness shifts with the seasons by similar amounts.

That is the core difficulty. A model learning to separate these two covers risks relying not on what genuinely tells them apart, but on details specific to the region where it learned: the particular hue of one soil, the haze of one season, the date of one acquisition.

Those details work perfectly where the model trained, and are worthless anywhere else. Hence the question that shaped the whole project: does this model still work in a region it has never seen?

The evaluation

How I evaluated the model

Answering that question takes more than the usual way of testing a model.

The usual way sets aside a share of the examples, drawn at random, and uses them as the exam. The trouble in remote sensing is that neighbouring plots resemble each other closely: same soil, same weather, often the same satellite image. With a random draw, near-identical plots land on both sides, and the model is scored on situations very close to ones it has already seen.

So I divided the territory into 100 km geographic blocks, and made sure each block sat entirely on one side of the split. The model learns on some regions and sits its exam on others, which it discovers.

The two ways of measuring give different numbers, and the gap between them is instructive.

The solution

What changed the result

Optical imagery measures colour. What was needed was something that measures shape.

Satellite radar does not photograph the landscape: it sends out a wave and listens to the echo. What it senses is the structure of the vegetation cover. A plantation has aligned trees of uniform height, and returns a regular echo. Natural forest is disordered, and so is its echo.

Where optical imagery saw no difference, radar measures one. It also has a practical advantage: it sees through clouds. Over a region that is overcast much of the year, that is the difference between usable data and no data at all.

With radar, and by grouping the question into two categories, palm or no palm, the score on unseen regions reaches 0.821, measured across five successive exams each covering different regions.

The maps

The maps also show what the model misses

A score summarises, it does not show. So I mapped the results of the evaluation.

These maps display the test points themselves, the ones on which performance was measured. Each dot is a plot, and its colour says what the model made of it.

ColourSituation
Palm detected there was palm, and the model saw it
Correctly excluded there was no palm, and the model reported none
False alarm there was no palm, and the model reported some
Palm missed there was palm, and the model did not see it

The first two situations are successes, the next two are errors. The errors are what would call for human verification, and making them visible is the reason the map exists.

The choice

Why I show the bad block

In the central basin, the model misses 6 palm plots out of 200. In South Kivu, it misses 50 out of 200.

Showing the bad block is deliberate. A model does not behave the same way everywhere, and presenting only the region where it shines would give a false picture of what to expect from it.

The gap between these two maps is useful information for anyone considering using it: it says that local verification remains necessary before trusting the model in a given area.

The numbers

What the numbers say

Now that the maps are readable, the numbers make sense. Across all test plots, spread over regions the model had never seen:

983palm plots found
107palm plots missed
146false alarms
554areas rightly excluded

Translated into practical use: out of 100 existing palm plots, the model flags 90. When it reports palm, it is right in roughly 87 cases out of 100.

That is exactly the profile of a prioritisation tool. It narrows the field to examine substantially, without closing it entirely. The 107 missed plots are a reminder that no signal is not proof of absence, and the 146 false alarms that a signal still needs confirming.

The field

What the ranking is worth

A score says whether the model is right. It does not say what you gain by using it. A mission lead asks something else: if I visit plots in the model's order, how many palm plots will I have found after a quarter of the visits?

So I ranked the 8,658 plots by decreasing probability, then counted. To find half the palm plots you have to walk through 32% of the list. Visiting at random, with no model, would take 50%. And a perfect ranking, one that put every palm plot at the top, would need 30%: over that stretch, the model sits two points off the optimum.

The gain then narrows. To find nine palm plots in ten you still need 67% of the list, against 90% at random. That is the expected behaviour of a prioritisation tool: it pays heavily on the first visits, and less and less as you push towards completeness.

This gain is not the same everywhere. Block by block, the share of palm plots flagged has a median of 90%, but three blocks out of 34 fall below 70%, the weakest at 58%. It is that spread, not the average, that decides whether the ranking is usable in a given region.

The prototype

What the list looks like on the ground

A ranked list is still a list. To prepare a round of visits you have to see it: where the priority plots are, whether they cluster, how much is ruled out from the start.

So I drew the prototype on one block, in Sud-Ubangi. Its 200 plots fall into three decisions rather than four confusion-matrix colours, because a round of visits has only three outcomes: go there first, look at it next, leave it. The upper threshold is 0.8, the lower one 0.4.

The right-hand panel is the safeguard for the left-hand one. A priority map on its own would suggest that a colour amounts to a confirmation. The bars state what each band actually holds: 95% palm above 0.8, 77% between 0.6 and 0.8, 53% between 0.4 and 0.6, 7% below 0.2. The sample average, 61%, gives the yardstick without which those figures mean nothing.

This is a prototype, not a tool. Its purpose is to settle what a mission map should be made of before building one.

The limits

What the model cannot do yet

This model is not yet built to fill in a continuous map of the DRC.

The reason lies in how it learned. Its no-palm category does not represent the full range of Congolese landscapes: it represents what surrounds palm plantations, which is mostly forest and neighbouring crops.

Applied to a whole territory, it would run into rivers, villages, bare soil, dry savanna, highlands. All environments it never had the chance to learn to rule out. Its behaviour there is unknown, and nothing supports a claim that it would be reliable.

Publishing a prediction layer covering the whole country would therefore be misleading, even with a disclaimer attached: the image would be handsome and the information wrong. The map of test points, by contrast, is honest, because it shows exactly the domain in which performance was measured.

Two further limits are worth knowing. The model does not tell an industrial plantation from a smallholder grove, because what separates them is plantation geometry rather than anything a satellite sees point by point. And the reference labels it learned from come from a mapping effort of their own, with its own errors, which Classi-fy inherits uncorrected.

Next

Four pieces of work, in this order

Broaden the non-palm environments

Teach the model to recognise savanna, towns, mountains and wetlands. This is what is missing most.

Honour how rare palm actually is

In my dataset palm is heavily present because sampling targeted the areas where it grows. Across the real territory it covers a fraction of a percent. A model meant to cover a country has to be trained and tuned in those proportions.

Add terrain and texture

Elevation and slope rule out areas where palm does not grow at all. Texture features, which describe how the canopy is spatially organised, are the only serious route to telling industrial plantations from smallholder groves.

Collect field labels

The only way to measure real performance rather than agreement with another mapping effort.

In closing

A tool that prepares, not one that replaces

Classi-fy helps prepare a human check: it points to the places to look at first and rules out a large share of the rest. It does not replace fieldwork, and on regions it has not learned, it says nothing reliable.

What I take from this project is less the number obtained than the way of obtaining it. Evaluating on disjoint regions costs a few points of headline score, and gives back something more useful: knowing where the model holds and where it does not.

Method

The detail, for anyone checking

References
Oil palm extent maps from Descals et al. 2024, published on Zenodo under CC BY 4.0, at 10 metre resolution and validated in the field.
Sample
8,658 points across 34 blocks of 100 km, between 12.1 and 30.1 degrees east and between -4.9 and 4.0 degrees north. This spans the width of the Congo Basin, with 24 blocks in the Democratic Republic of the Congo and 10 across six neighbouring countries.
Imagery
Sentinel-2 for optical, six bands from visible to shortwave infrared, clouds and shadows masked. Sentinel-1 for radar, VV and VH polarisations. Reference year 2021. One value per month and per band, that is 96 measurements per point.
Features
34 annual features: vegetation indices and their statistics, per-band means and variability, and the gap between the two radar polarisations, the most informative of them all.
Evaluation
Spatial block cross-validation, five folds, the group being the 100 km block. Headline score macro F1 of 0.821, standard deviation 0.015 across the five folds. A model that always answered the majority category would score 0.378 on the same data.
Models
Random forest and gradient boosting land within five thousandths of each other, which indicates that performance comes from the chosen features rather than the algorithm.
Tools
Python, Google Earth Engine, scikit-learn, geopandas, rasterio, pandas.

Sources: Descals, A. et al. (2024). Global Oil Palm extent maps. Zenodo, 10.5281/zenodo.13379129, CC BY 4.0. Copernicus Sentinel-1 and Sentinel-2, European Space Agency.
